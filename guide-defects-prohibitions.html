Defects and prohibitions

Defects are inevitable. What matters is how quickly they're reported, assessed and fixed. Here's how defects should be handled day to day, and how DVSA examiners and MOT testers grade them.

Great BritainLast checked 22 October 2026

Reporting and fixing defects

  • Drivers must report any defects, or symptoms of defects, that could stop the vehicle operating safely. That includes faults that show up on the road, such as warning lights or vibrations.
  • Every report goes to a responsible person with the authority to act on it, which can mean taking the vehicle out of service.
  • Anything that makes the vehicle unroadworthy must be fixed before it's used.
  • Defect reports, with the assessment and repair details, are part of the maintenance record and must be kept for at least 15 months. Electronic records are fine.

What a defect report should record

The vehicle registration or ID, the date, the defects or symptoms, who reported them and who they were reported to, the assessment, the repair work, and the date it was completed.

Nil defect reports

DVSA recommends "nil" reporting, where the driver confirms the check was done and nothing was found, and keeping those reports for at least 3 months. They prove checks are happening, and they can make a real difference at a roadside check (see the concession below).

Roadside checks: how DVSA grades defects

OutcomeWhat it means
Immediate prohibitionDriving the vehicle any further would risk injury, so it can't be used until it's fixed.
Delayed prohibitionThe ban starts later, up to 10 days after the inspection, to allow time for repairs.
Inspection noticeAdvisory only, so it doesn't stop the vehicle being used. But some advisory defects still mean the vehicle doesn't meet the law, so fix them as soon as practicable.

How long a delayed prohibition gives you

BandDelayUsually given for
AUp to 10 daysFewer than 5 defects, none in safety-critical areas
B4 to 7 days1 defect in a safety-critical area, or 5 or more in other areas
CUp to 3 daysMore than 1 defect in a safety-critical area

Safety-critical means anything that could affect the control or directional stability of the vehicle. A prohibition stays in force until an examiner is satisfied the vehicle is fit for service and removes it (on form PG10). Defects with brakes, steering or tyres can also lead to fixed penalties and penalty points for the driver.

"S" and "X" markings

Examiners also mark each prohibition defect for what it says about your maintenance:

MarkingMeaning
SA significant failure of roadworthiness compliance. For example, a long-standing defect that should have been found at the last safety inspection or the daily walkaround check.
BlankMay or may not be down to poor maintenance.
XNo reflection on maintenance, such as a bulb failure the driver couldn't have noticed.
Why S-markings matter

Every prohibition goes to the Traffic Commissioner, and an S-marking points at your maintenance system rather than bad luck.

The delayed defect concession

An examiner can downgrade a delayed prohibition to an inspection notice if the driver can show the defect happened on the current day's journey and the vehicle was clear at the start, backed by a nil defect report. There must also be either an in-service defect report assessing the vehicle as safe to finish the day's journey, or evidence it's heading straight to be repaired. It doesn't apply if any other prohibition defect is found, and the driver must provide the evidence at the time.

MOT results

ResultWhat to do

Major (fail)Could affect safety, put other road users at risk or affect the environment. Repair it immediately.
Minor (pass)No significant effect on safety or the environment. Repair it as soon as possible.
AdvisoryCould become a problem. Monitor it and repair if necessary.

Good practice

  • Track every defect through to a signed-off repair.
  • Look for repeat defects on the same vehicle or model. They often point to a maintenance or driver-training problem.
